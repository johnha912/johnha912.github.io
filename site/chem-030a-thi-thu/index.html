Atoms, isotopes and ions

0:00 → 0:15 · 15 min

About 21 of the practice exam's points; the base for naming ions in the next block.

The three particles

ParticleChargeMass (amu)Where
Proton+11.0073Nucleus
Neutron01.0087Nucleus
Electron−10.0005Outside the nucleus

Protons and neutrons carry almost all the mass; the electrons take up almost all the space. An atom is the smallest piece of an element that keeps its properties.

Read the symbol AZXcharge

  • Z, atomic number = protons. It decides the element.
  • A, mass number = protons + neutrons.
  • Neutrons = A − Z.
  • Electrons = Z − charge. A neutral atom has electrons = protons.
Worked: count everything (instructor notes)
  • 12753I: p 53, n 127 − 53 = 74, e 53. As I− it gains one electron: e 54.
  • 8838Sr: p 38, n 50, e 38. As Sr2+ it loses two: e 36.
  • Al3+: p 13, n 14, e 10. N3−: p 7, n 7, e 10. O2−: p 8, n 8, e 10.

Ions

An ion is a charged atom. Cation (+): lost electrons; metals do this (Na+, Sr2+). Anion (−): gained electrons; nonmetals do this (Cl−, O2−). Neutrons never change the charge. Opposite charges attract, and the attraction weakens as they move apart.

Isotopes and average atomic mass

Isotopes: same element (same protons), different numbers of neutrons, so different mass numbers. Carbon-12, -13 and -14 have 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. The mass on the periodic table is a weighted average of the isotopes:

average mass = (fraction1 × mass1) + (fraction2 × mass2) + …

Worked: copper (instructor notes) Cu-63 is 69.09% at 62.9298 amu; Cu-65 is 30.91% at 64.9278 amu.
(0.6909 × 62.9298) + (0.3091 × 64.9278) = 43.48 + 20.07 = 63.55 amu.

Patterns that shrink the list

  • -ate has one more O than -ite, same charge: nitrate NO3− / nitrite NO2−; sulfate SO42− / sulfite SO32−.
  • per- adds one O to -ate; hypo- removes one O from -ite: ClO4− perchlorate, ClO3− chlorate, ClO2− chlorite, ClO− hypochlorite.
  • Adding H+ raises the charge by one and adds "hydrogen" (or "bi-"): CO32− → HCO3−; PO43− → HPO42− → H2PO4−.
Traps. Sulfide S2−, sulfite SO32− and sulfate SO42− are three different ions; the same goes for nitride, nitrite and nitrate. Ammonium NH4+ is the one common polyatomic cation. Mercury(I) is the pair Hg22+.

Naming ionic compounds

0:35 → 1:05 · 30 min

The largest block on the practice exam: name 6 compounds, write 8 formulas, plus MC items. Get this perfect.

Ionic = metal + nonmetal, or any compound with a polyatomic ion (NH4Cl). Name the cation first, then the anion. No prefixes, ever.

Formula from a name: make the charges add to zero

  1. Write each ion with its charge.
  2. Take the smallest whole numbers that make the total charge zero (cross the charges over).
  3. Put parentheses around a polyatomic ion when you need more than one.
Worked
  • Magnesium phosphate: Mg2+, PO43− → 3(+2) + 2(−3) = 0 → Mg3(PO4)2 (instructor note)
  • Copper(II) phosphate: Cu2+, PO43− → Cu3(PO4)2
  • Aluminum chromate: Al3+, CrO42− → 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0 → Al2(CrO4)3
  • Ammonium phosphate → (NH4)3PO4; zinc sulfate → ZnSO4; potassium sulfide → K2S
  • Copper(I) oxide: two Cu+ balance one O2− → Cu2O; tin(IV) fluoride → SnF4

Name from a formula

Metal with more than one chargeMetal (Roman numeral = its charge) + anionCuBr2 copper(II) bromide; FeS iron(II) sulfide

Worked: find the hidden charge (instructor note, Sep 13)
  • Pb(SO4)2: 2 × (−2) = −4, so Pb is +4 → lead(IV) sulfate
  • Pb(C2H3O2)4: 4 × (−1) = −4 → lead(IV) acetate
  • CrCl3: 3 × (−1) → Cr3+ → chromium(III) chloride
  • Cu(ClO3)2 → copper(II) chlorate; FePO4 → PO43− so Fe3+ → iron(III) phosphate (practice key)

Older "classical" names: the lower charge ends in -ous, the higher in -ic: cuprous/cupric, ferrous/ferric, stannous/stannic, plumbous/plumbic.

Traps. The Roman numeral is the metal's charge, not its subscript: Fe2O3 is iron(III) oxide. No Roman numeral for fixed-charge metals: K2S is potassium sulfide, not potassium(II) sulfide. No prefixes in ionic names: Al2O3 is not dialuminum trioxide. KC2H3O2 is potassium acetate, not calcium (K is potassium). Sodium bicarbonate is NaHCO3, not Na2CO3.

Molecular (covalent) = two nonmetals: use prefixes

Writing and balancing equations

1:45 → 2:10 · 25 min

Three balancing problems at 7 points each on the practice exam, plus an MC item. Also the first step of every stoichiometry problem.

Reactants → products. State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

Rules (Balancing page)

  1. Write the correct formulas first.
  2. Never change subscripts; they set what the substance is.
  3. Change only coefficients.
  4. Balance one element at a time: metals first, nonmetals next, H and O last.
  5. A polyatomic ion that appears unchanged on both sides is balanced as one unit.
  6. If needed, use a fraction, then multiply everything to clear it.
  7. Count every atom on both sides to check.
Worked: propane (Balancing page)
  1. C3H8 + O2 → CO2 + H2O
  2. C: 3 on the left → 3 CO2
  3. H: 8 on the left → 4 H2O
  4. O on the right: 3(2) + 4(1) = 10 → 5 O2
  5. C3H8 + 5 O2 → 3 CO2 + 4 H2O
Worked: polyatomic ion as a unit (practice key) K3PO4 + Ca(NO3)2 → KNO3 + Ca3(PO4)2. Two PO4 on the right → 2 K3PO4 → 6 K → 6 KNO3 → 6 NO3 → 3 Ca(NO3)2.
2 K3PO4 + 3 Ca(NO3)2 → 6 KNO3 + Ca3(PO4)2
Worked: clear a fraction (HW Ch7 Q3) C2H6 + O2 → 2 CO2 + 3 H2O needs 7 O on the left = 7/2 O2. Multiply all by 2: 2 C2H6 + 7 O2 → 4 CO2 + 6 H2O.

More answers from the kit: 2 H2 + O2 → 2 H2O; 2 Hg + O2 → 2 HgO; 4 Al + 3 O2 → 2 Al2O3; CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O; 2 Al + 3 H2SO4 → Al2(SO4)3 + 3 H2; C5H12 + 8 O2 → 5 CO2 + 6 H2O; P4 + 5 O2 → P4O10 (sum 7); 4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3 (sum 9).

Traps. Reduce to the lowest whole numbers: 2, 4, 2, 8 balances N2H4 + H2O2 → N2 + H2O, but the best answer is 1, 2, 1, 4. A "sum of coefficients" counts every 1. Hydrogen gas is H2, never H3. Fix wrong formulas before balancing.

Precipitates in the kit

A precipitate forms when two aqueous ionic compounds make a new compound that does not dissolve. The kit's insoluble solids: AgCl, PbI2, PbCO3, CaCO3. If every product is (aq), there is no precipitate (2 K2S2O3 + I2 → K2S4O6 + 2 KI is not precipitation).

Physical change is not a reaction: H2O(s) → H2O(l) is melting. Products of a metal plus acid: Al + H2SO4 gives aluminum sulfate and hydrogen gas. Copper placed in aluminum bromide does not react.

Traps. Fuel + O2 making CO2 and H2O is combustion, not combination. Neutralization and precipitation are both double replacements. One reactant splitting = decomposition, even when O2 is a product.

Stoichiometry

2:35 → 3:05 · 30 min

On the study guide: moles, grams, number of particles (Avogadro's number), plus limiting reactant and percent yield from the Chapter 8 worksheet.

The one road

grams A÷ molar mass Amoles A× mole ratiomoles B× molar mass Bgrams B

Particles: moles × 6.022 × 1023 (Avogadro's number, given on the exam). The mole ratio comes from the balanced equation's coefficients and only works on moles.

Molar mass = add the atomic masses

H2O: 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol. CaCO3: 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g/mol. Read atomic masses off the periodic table you are given. Values used in this app:

Worked: moles to grams (worksheet Q1) CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O. Grams of water from 0.264 mol CH4?
0.264 mol CH4 × (2 mol H2O / 1 mol CH4) × 18.02 g/mol = 9.51 g H2O
Worked: mass to mass (worksheet Q2) CaCN2 + 3 H2O → CaCO3 + 2 NH3. Kilograms of NH3 from 25.0 kg CaCN2?
25.0 kg ÷ 80.11 = 0.312 kmol × 2 = 0.624 kmol NH3 × 17.03 = 10.6 kg NH3. Kilograms in, kilograms out: kmol works like mol.
Worked: particles (worksheet Q7) 4 NH3 + 7 O2 → 4 NO2 + 6 H2O. 5.38 mol NH3 → 5.38 mol NO2 × 6.022 × 1023 = 3.24 × 1024 molecules

Limiting reactant

The limiting reactant runs out first; when it is gone the reaction stops, so it sets how much product forms. The other reactant is in excess and some is left over. Method: turn each reactant into moles, then ask how much of the other one it needs.

Worked: worksheet Q9 (key: 20.2 g CO2) CaCO3 + 2 HCl → CaCl2 + CO2 + H2O with 45.9 g CaCO3 and 51.6 g HCl.
CaCO3: 45.9 ÷ 100.09 = 0.459 mol, needs 0.917 mol HCl. HCl present: 51.6 ÷ 36.46 = 1.42 mol, plenty. CaCO3 limits.
0.459 mol CO2 × 44.01 = 20.2 g CO2
Worked: excess left over (worksheet Q10) N2 + 3 H2 → 2 NH3 with 1.40 g N2 (0.0500 mol) and 0.400 g H2 (0.198 mol). N2 needs 0.150 mol H2, so N2 limits and 0.048 mol H2 is left = about 0.10 g H2 (the key's 0.100 g uses rounded masses; exact table values give 0.098 g).

Percent yield

percent yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100. Theoretical yield is what the stoichiometry predicts from the limiting reactant.

Worked: worksheet Q4 and Q5
  • 1.376 g is a 78.5% yield: theoretical = 1.376 ÷ 0.785 = 1.753 g.
  • 2 Sb + 3 Cl2 → 2 SbCl3 at 88.3%; you want 4.07 g SbCl3. Plan for 4.07 ÷ 0.883 = 4.61 g ÷ 228.11 = 0.0202 mol SbCl3 × 3/2 = 0.0303 mol Cl2 × 70.90 = 2.15 g Cl2.
Traps. Never put grams into a mole ratio. Diatomic gases use the doubled mass: O2 32.00, H2 2.016, Cl2 70.90, I2 253.80. The limiting reactant is not always the one with the smaller mass. Percent yield cannot be above 100%.

Light and electron configurations

3:15 → 3:35 · 20 min

Three configurations at 5 points each, plus MC items. "Be able to write the electron configuration of any given atom."

Light in one line

Wavelength λ = peak-to-peak distance; frequency ν = waves per second. c = λν, c = 3.00 × 108 m/s (given). Longer wavelength means lower frequency.

Worked (Quiz Ch9 Q15) Orange light, 620 nm = 620 × 10−9 m. ν = 3.00 × 108 ÷ 6.20 × 10−7 = 4.8 × 1014 s−1. Convert nm to m first.

The lowest energy state of an electron is the ground state; higher ones are excited states.

Each orbital holds 2 electrons with opposite spins. Energy level n = 2 holds 8 (2s + 2p); n = 3 holds 18 (3s + 3p + 3d).

Filling order

1s→2s→2p→3s→3p→4s→3d→4p

Fill each sublevel before the next. The superscripts must add up to the number of electrons.

Worked
  • Na (11): 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s1. N (7): 1s2 2s2 2p3.
  • Cl (17): 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p5
  • Fe (26): 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d6
  • Br atom (35): 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6 4s2 3d10 4p5. Bromide ion Br− (36): ends 4p6.
  • Abbreviated Kr (36): [Ar] 4s2 3d10 4p6. The bracket replaces the core; do not write 1s…3p again (the practice key does, and that is an error).

Ions: anions add electrons, cations remove them. Ca2+, K+ and Cl− all have 18 electrons: 1s2 2s2 2p6 3s2 3p6.

Valence electrons and blocks

Valence electrons are in the outermost level; for main-group elements they equal the A-group number: alkali metals 1, alkaline earth 2, Al 3, N and P 5, O and S 6, halogens 7, noble gases 8. Same group = same valence electrons; same period = same number of energy levels. The block is the sublevel filled last: [Ar] 4s2 3d8 is d block; …3p2 is p block.

Traps. 4s fills before 3d. Sulfur has 6 valence electrons, not 16. In the third level of Cl there are 7 electrons (3s2 3p5). Check that your superscripts add to the electron count.
