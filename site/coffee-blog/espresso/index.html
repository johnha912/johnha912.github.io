Espresso

Do I really need to introduce this?

Just kidding. Espresso is a concentrated brewing method that uses high pressure to push hot water through finely ground coffee, creating a rich, full-bodied shot topped with crema. Developed in Italy, it’s typically brewed in about 25–30 seconds, producing an intense, complex flavor in a small serving (Illy, n.d.; Rao, 2019). It’s the foundation of drinks like lattes, cappuccinos, and Americanos.

Is it easy to pull a perfect one?

Of course not! A perfect espresso is a notoriously elusive goal that demands precision and a deep understanding of technical factors that are hard to control consistently. Small variations in grind size, tamping pressure, or water temperature can throw off extraction, leaving a shot sour, bitter, or unbalanced. Even with advanced equipment, holding the roughly 9 bars of pressure needed for even extraction is challenging: high-end machines can still fluctuate, which disrupts flavor and crema (Daily Coffee News, 2019).

The process is unforgiving: small mistakes amplify into real defects. Mastering espresso takes knowledge, skill, near-obsessive attention to detail, and a willingness to keep adjusting. That is why great espresso remains an art few can reliably achieve.

Step-by-step guide by Illy

  1. Prepare and dose. Weigh and grind the coffee to a fine consistency that holds up under pressure without over-extracting (Illy, n.d.).
  2. Tamp. Distribute the coffee evenly in the portafilter and apply firm, consistent pressure for a compact puck, so water flows evenly (Hoffmann, 2018).
  3. Brew. Start the extraction and aim for 25–30 seconds for a balanced shot. If the time is off, adjust grind size or tamping (Rao, 2019).
  4. Observe. The shot should flow smoothly with crema. A fast flow calls for a finer grind; a slow flow, a coarser one (Hoffmann, 2018).

TDS: the number behind the taste

Beyond pressure and grind size, Total Dissolved Solids (TDS) plays a crucial role in extraction. TDS measures the concentration of dissolved particles, which drives a shot’s strength, flavor intensity, and mouthfeel. An espresso typically lands around 8–12% TDS, depending on preference and the coffee itself (Correia, Soares, Tribess, & Oliveira, 2019). At a fixed brew ratio, high TDS often signals over-extraction (bitter and heavy), while low TDS can mean under-extraction (sour or weak). Controlling TDS alongside the other variables is what makes a consistently rich, balanced espresso.

Strictly speaking, TDS measures strength. Extraction yield (how much of the dry coffee ended up in the cup) is calculated from it: extraction yield = TDS × beverage weight ÷ dose. A brewing control chart plots the two together. Once you pick a brew ratio, every shot you pull lands on that ratio’s line: grind and time move you along the line, and only a new ratio moves you to another one (Barista Hustle, n.d.; Pilot Coffee Roasters, n.d.).
